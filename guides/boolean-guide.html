Writing strong booleans for brand & industry searches

A practical reference for constructing brand, competitor, and industry booleans that actually hold up, built from real searches set up for real customers, not theory.

Guide last updated: September 23, 2026

Context

How to use this guide

This isn't a pitch for why boolean quality matters. You already know that. It's a technique reference: how to actually construct a brand boolean and an industry boolean that hold up, built from real searches set up for real customers.

Two search types, same discipline. A brand boolean tracks mentions of a specific company, product, or organization. An industry/topic boolean tracks a subject area or intersection of subjects, no brand name required. Competitor searches use the exact same construction logic as brand searches; they're covered as a sub-case below, not a separate skill.
Brand & Competitor Searches

Building a brand boolean

Every brand boolean is solving one problem: capture every real mention of the brand while excluding everything the name, handle, or domain could also mean. How much work that takes depends entirely on how ambiguous, and how big, the brand is. Competitor searches are covered here too, not as a separate topic: they use the exact same toolkit, just aimed at a different company (see the callout partway through the examples below).

Name variants & punctuation

Cover the way people actually type the name: hyphens, apostrophes, spacing, wildcard suffixes ("Trader Joe*"), and the squashed-together or hashtag form ("TraderJoes", "bucees").

Disambiguate with NEAR / ONEAR

When the bare name is a generic word or common phrase, pair it with context terms inside a proximity window. Use NEAR when order doesn't matter, ONEAR when it does.

Exact-match signals

Full formal name, official social handles, website domain, containsLink to that domain, and retail-partner listing URLs if the brand sells through marketplaces. These need no disambiguation: they can only mean the brand.

Source-level targeting

Sometimes the cleanest signal isn't a keyword at all. Pull an entire source wholesale (sourceName:/r/brand) when you can safely assume everything there is on-topic.

Image text

imageText: catches product and merchandise photos where the brand name is visible in the image but may not appear in the surrounding text or caption.

Sub-brands & private label

Don't stop at the corporate name. Capture product lines and store brands that are just as identifying (e.g. "Kirkland Signature" for Costco).

Scope the boolean to the brand's size and ambiguity, not a template. A small brand with a distinctive or ambiguous name (Venable, Forest Ridge) needs a tighter, more surgical boolean built around disambiguation. A large, ubiquitous consumer brand (Trader Joe's) is better served by a deliberately broad catch-all: capture everything, then narrow downstream with custom categories (product lines, seasonal campaigns, specific launches) rather than trying to make the boolean itself narrow.
Small / ambiguous name Venable LLP: legal services venable.com ↗
Boolean
(Venable^8 NEAR/12 (legal OR lawyer* OR attorney*)) OR ("Venable LLP"^7 OR VenableLLP OR "venable.com") OR (Venable AND topic:legal_services)
When a brand name is also a common word, don't rely on the name alone. Pair it with context terms inside a NEAR window, then add an unambiguous exact-match layer as backup.
Read the reasoning

"Venable" alone is a generic-sounding word used in plenty of contexts that have nothing to do with this law firm, so the boolean has to do real disambiguation work.

The first line pairs the bare name with core legal terms inside a 12-word proximity window: if "Venable" shows up near "legal," "lawyer," or "attorney" language, it's very likely this brand. The second line is exact-match: the full firm name, the "VenableLLP" handle used consistently across their social channels, and their domain. No disambiguation is needed here because these can only mean the firm. The third line is a broader catch-all: any mention of the bare name inside a document already tagged legal_services. It picked up a handful more true positives the first two lines missed.

Large / ubiquitous brand Trader Joe's: grocery retail
Boolean
("Trader Joe*"^7 OR "TraderJoes"^7 OR "traderjoes.com" OR containsLink:"traderjoes.com") OR SourceName:/r/traderjoes OR (imageText:trader AND (imageText:joe's OR imageText:joes)) OR ((TJs) AND (topic:food_and_grocery_retailers OR topic:food))
Boolean robustness should scale with brand size. A ubiquitous consumer brand gets a broad catch-all; let custom categories do the narrowing, not the boolean.
Read the reasoning

Trader Joe's is big enough that the goal isn't disambiguation. It's building a deliberately broad catch-all, then narrowing downstream with custom categories (Tote Bags, Fall Items, a specific product) instead of trying to make the boolean itself narrow.

Line one covers keyword variants with a wildcard ("Trader Joe*" catches Trader Joe, Trader Joes, Trader Joe's), plus the website and the one-word social handle/hashtag form. Line two pulls the entire r/traderjoes subreddit wholesale, on the assumption that everything there is relevant. Line three catches product and merchandise photos where "Trader Joe's" appears in the image itself but may not be in the surrounding text. Line four handles the shorthand "TJ's": since an acronym alone is too ambiguous to run unpaired, it's grounded with relevant topic tags.

Punctuation handling Buc-ee's: travel centers
Boolean
"Buc-ees"^8 OR "Buc?ees" OR @bucees OR "bucees"
If a brand name has a hyphen or apostrophe, cover the punctuated form, the wildcard form, and the plain squashed-together form people actually type.
Read the reasoning

A small, clean example of handling punctuation variance. The name has both a hyphen and an apostrophe, and real people type it inconsistently: with the hyphen, with the apostrophe swapped for a wildcard character, or squashed together entirely. No NEAR or topic disambiguation is needed here since "Buc-ee's" isn't a word used any other way.

Misspellings & retail URLs Owala: water bottles
Boolean
(Owala OR @owala OR owalalife.com OR sourceName:/r/owala) OR (Owalla) OR ("target.com/b/owala")
Check for the brand's common misspellings, and don't stop at the brand's own domain. Retail-partner listing pages are a real source of mentions for brands that sell through marketplaces.
Read the reasoning

Two techniques beyond the basics here. First, a common misspelling, "Owalla" with a double L, gets its own clause, since a meaningful share of real mentions use it. Second, since Owala sells through third-party retailers, the boolean includes the brand's specific product listing path on Target's own site (target.com/b/owala), not just Owala's own domain.

Small / niche name Forest Ridge School of the Sacred Heart: K-12 school forestridge.org ↗
Boolean
("Forest Ridge School" ONEAR/2 "Sacred Heart") OR @ForestRidgeSchool OR forestridge.org OR (("Forest Ridge School") NEAR/12 (Bellevue OR Catholic))
ONEAR enforces word order, NEAR doesn't. Disambiguation terms don't have to be industry vocabulary either. Geography and other context words work just as well when that's what actually makes a name unambiguous.
Read the reasoning

"Forest Ridge School" alone is generic enough that it needs grounding, but the fix here isn't industry vocabulary the way Venable used legal terms. It's the school's full institutional name and its geography.

The first clause uses ONEAR (ordered proximity, where the phrases must appear in that specific sequence) to bind "Forest Ridge School" to "Sacred Heart" within 2 words, since the full name is what's actually unambiguous. That's backed by the official handle and domain. The second clause is a second disambiguation layer: the school name paired with geographic and context terms (Bellevue, Catholic) inside a wider NEAR window, for mentions that don't use the full formal name.

Long name, no disambiguation needed Asheville Regional Airport
Boolean
"Asheville Regional Airport" OR "Greater Asheville Regional Airport" OR "FlyAVL" OR flyavl.com OR "Asheville Rgnl Airport" OR "Asheville airport"
Not every brand needs a disambiguation layer. If the full name is already unambiguous, focus your energy on covering real-world variants instead.
Read the reasoning

Sometimes the name is already specific enough that no proximity operator or topic catch-all is needed at all. Just cover the realistic variants. That's the formal name, the "Greater" variant sometimes used, the brand's own public-facing handle ("FlyAVL"), the domain, and the way people actually abbreviate or misspell it ("Rgnl" for "Regional").

Short / ambiguous name AYR: apparel ayr.com ↗
Boolean
(caseSensitive:(AYR^7) AND language:en AND topic:apparel) OR (ayr.com OR containsLink:"ayr.com*" OR "@AYR")
caseSensitive: is another disambiguation tool alongside NEAR/ONEAR, useful when a short, all-caps name would otherwise collide with a common lowercase word. language: and other filters can also live inline in the boolean itself, not just as external search filters.
Read the reasoning

A three-letter all-caps name is about as ambiguous as it gets: "AYR" collides with the plain word "ayr," the Scottish town of Ayr, and plenty of other noise. Rather than solving that with NEAR + industry terms (Venable) or NEAR + geography (Forest Ridge), this one uses a third approach: caseSensitive forces an exact-case match on "AYR", paired with topic:apparel to ground it in the right industry and language:en to keep it to English-language mentions. The second clause is the usual exact-match layer: domain, a wildcarded containsLink covering any URL path under the domain, and the social handle.

Hardest case · three techniques stacked RideAlso ("Also"): micromobility ridealso.com ↗
Boolean
(RideAlso OR ridealso.com OR @ridealso OR @ride_also OR SourceName:/r/ALSOmicromobility/) OR (Also NEAR/2 ("TB-M" OR "alpha wave" OR alphawave)) OR (caseSensitive:(ALSO) AND topic:bicycles_and_accessories)
Disambiguation terms can come from three places: industry vocabulary (Venable), geography (Forest Ridge), or the brand's own proprietary product names (RideAlso). For the most ambiguous names, stack more than one technique rather than relying on just one.
Read the reasoning

The brand's actual product name is the bare word "Also" (about as ambiguous a name as exists), marketed combined as "RideAlso." That takes all three disambiguation techniques covered so far, stacked into one boolean.

Line one is the exact-match cluster: the combined brand name, domain, both handle variants (with and without the underscore), and the brand's subreddit pulled in wholesale. Line two pairs the bare word "Also" with specific product/model terms ("TB-M", "alpha wave") inside a tight 2-word window. That's a new source for disambiguation terms: proprietary product vocabulary, alongside Venable's industry terms and Forest Ridge's geography. Line three is the same caseSensitive + topic combo as AYR: matching only the all-caps stylized "ALSO" and grounding it in topic:bicycles_and_accessories.

Acronym · multiple techniques stacked American Board of Internal Medicine (ABIM) abim.org ↗
Boolean
("American Board of Internal Medicine"^7 OR ("American Board" ONEAR/1 "Internal Medicine")) OR (abim.org OR @abimcert OR abim.certification) OR (caseSensitive:(ABIM) AND topic:health) OR (caseSensitive:(ABIM) NEAR/6 (Credit* OR Certificat* OR point*))
caseSensitive isn't limited to one secondary grounding. Running it in parallel against both a topic clause and a NEAR+vocabulary clause maximizes recall on an ambiguous acronym while keeping precision on each individual clause.
Read the reasoning

Four clauses, each solving a different angle of this acronym-heavy name. Line one matches the full formal name exactly, and also covers it split with a 1-word gap tolerance ("American Board" ONEAR/1 "Internal Medicine"), a tight ordered-proximity trick that catches minor variation in how the connecting word between the two name fragments renders, without needing a full wildcard. Line two is the usual exact-match cluster: domain, handle, and a certification-portal-style string.

Lines three and four both disambiguate the bare acronym "ABIM" with caseSensitive, but ground it two different ways in parallel rather than picking one: line three pairs it with topic:health (the same caseSensitive+topic combo seen with AYR and RideAlso), while line four pairs it with NEAR/6 against wildcarded vocabulary specific to how this acronym actually gets discussed: certification credits and points.

Genuine name collision · NOT in action CNIC (Commander, Navy Installations Command) cnic.navy.mil ↗
Boolean
(caseSensitive:(CNIC) AND topic:military NOT ("Computerised National Identity Card" OR Pakista* OR "CNIC Card")) OR ("Commander" NEAR/1 "Navy Installations Command") OR (cnic.navy.mil OR cnichq)
This is what a targeted, name-collision NOT actually looks like in practice: narrow and specific to the one colliding entity, not a general noise filter. See Exclusions & Noise below for when this applies vs. when to use the Implementation Cloner System instead.
Read the reasoning

This is the real-world case the Exclusions guidance below is talking about: "CNIC" also commonly refers to Pakistan's national ID card ("Computerised National Identity Card"), a completely unrelated entity, not just background noise. So line one pairs the now-familiar caseSensitive + topic combo (a 4th example of this recurring pattern, after AYR, RideAlso, and ABIM) with a narrow, targeted NOT that excludes only that specific collision: the spelled-out card name, a wildcarded "Pakista*", and the phrase "CNIC Card."

Line two binds the organization's actual formal name, "Commander" and "Navy Installations Command," inside a 1-word gap using plain NEAR rather than ONEAR. Order isn't forced here, unlike ABIM's ONEAR/1, since either fragment could reasonably lead. Line three is the exact-match cluster: the official .mil domain and the organization's handle.

Ambiguous name · product-category grounding Quest Nutrition: protein snacks questnutrition.com ↗
Boolean
("Quest Nutrition"^8 OR questnutrition.com OR questnutrition) OR ((Quest^7) NEAR/2 ("Protein Chip*" OR "Protein Bar*" OR "Hero Bar*" OR "protein shake*" OR "protein powder" OR "protein milk*"))
When disambiguating a generic-word brand name, consider grounding in the product categories the brand sells, not just industry vocabulary. It's often the more natural and complete list.
Read the reasoning

Line one is the exact-match cluster: full brand name, domain, squashed handle form. Line two disambiguates the bare word "Quest" (a generic word, and a name plenty of other companies use, Quest Diagnostics among them) inside a tight 2-word window against wildcarded product-category terms: protein chips, bars, a specific product line ("Hero Bar"), shakes, powder, and milk.

This is a fourth flavor of disambiguation-grounding vocabulary, alongside Venable's industry terms, Forest Ridge's geography, and RideAlso's proprietary codenames: here it's the brand's own product categories: what it's actually known for selling. For an ambiguous-name CPG brand, that's usually more reliable and repeatable than hunting for internal codenames.

Multi-vertical brand · ambiguous foreign-language name UOVO: art storage, logistics, fashion & wine uovo.com ↗
Boolean
(caseSensitive:("UOVO"^7) AND (topic:visual_art_and_design OR topic:transportation_and_logistics OR topic:fashion_and_style)) OR ("uovo.com" OR "uovo.art" OR "uovofineart" OR uovofashion OR uovo.fashion OR uovo.wine OR uovo-wine) OR (caseSensitive:("UOVO") NEAR/6 (art OR fashion OR wine OR storage OR store OR shipping OR galler*))
A brand operating across several unrelated business lines needs its exact-match and vocabulary layers to cover every vertical, not just the primary one. One domain and one grounding category aren't enough here.
Read the reasoning

"UOVO" is Italian for "egg," a generic foreign-language word, and the brand itself spans four distinct business lines: fine art storage, logistics, fashion, and wine. That combination, an ambiguous name plus multiple unrelated verticals, needs more coverage than a single-category brand.

Line one pairs caseSensitive with an OR cluster of topics rather than one topic tag, since no single topic covers art storage, logistics, and fashion at once. Line two is the exact-match cluster, but instead of one consistent domain it lists a separate domain or handle per business line: uovo.com, uovo.art for the art-storage side, uovofineart and uovofashion as handles, plus uovo.fashion, uovo.wine, and the hyphenated uovo-wine variant. Line three repeats the caseSensitive disambiguation, this time grounded with NEAR/6 against vocabulary spanning every vertical at once: art, fashion, wine, storage, and shipping terms together, rather than one product category the way Quest Nutrition's grounding worked.

Public figure / government official Lt. Governor Deidre Henderson (Utah) ltgovernor.utah.gov ↗
Boolean
(("Lt." OR Lieutenant) NEAR/1 (Gov. OR Governor) NEAR/3 (Utah OR Henderson OR Deidre)) OR ("Office of the Lieutenant Governor" NEAR/2 (Utah OR Henderson OR Deidre)) OR (ltgovernor.utah.gov OR lt.govhenderson OR lghendersonutah OR deidrehendersonutah)
Chain NEAR windows when a generic title needs two layers of grounding: first binding title variants together, then requiring nearby identifying context. And don't assume a person's handle is consistent across platforms the way a brand's usually is.
Read the reasoning

Searches for a prominent individual use the same toolkit as brand searches. It's just the "brand" that's a person. The title "Lieutenant Governor" alone is useless on its own; every state has one. So this chains two proximity windows: first binding the title's abbreviation variants together ("Lt." or "Lieutenant" NEAR/1 "Gov." or "Governor"), then requiring that whole title phrase to land within 3 words of identifying context: the state, the last name, or the first name. The second line does the same for the formal office name, which is equally generic without that context.

The exact-match line carries a wrinkle specific to people rather than brands: a public figure often has inconsistent handle conventions across platforms: three genuinely different naming logics here (lt.govhenderson, lghendersonutah, deidrehendersonutah), rather than the one consistent handle a company typically reuses everywhere.

From here: competitor searches. A competitor search isn't a different discipline. It's a brand search for a different company, usually set up inside your customer's own account. Same techniques, same toolkit: name variants, exact-match signals, source targeting, image text. Depth can be lighter, though: a competitor search usually just needs to track mentions, sentiment, and moves, not the same exhaustive catch-all-plus-custom-categories depth as the account's own brand, unless the customer wants that level of competitive intelligence.
Competitor · set up in Trader Joe's account Costco
Boolean
(Costco^8 OR @Costco OR costco.com OR containsLink:costco.com) OR sourceName:/r/Costco OR imageText:"Costco" OR "Kirkland Signature"
We'd build a similar boolean if Costco were our own customer. Competitor and brand searches aren't different skills.
Read the reasoning

Same building blocks as Trader Joe's own boolean (weighted name, handle, domain, subreddit, image text), but lighter, since "Costco" isn't an ambiguous word the way "Venable" or the "TJ's" shorthand is. The one addition worth noting: "Kirkland Signature," Costco's private-label brand, included alongside the corporate name, the same sub-brand logic covered above.

Competitor · nearby airport Greenville-Spartanburg International Airport
Boolean
"Greenville-Spartanburg International Airport" OR "Greenville Spartanburg International Airport" OR "Greenville-Spartanburg Airport" OR "Greenville Spartanburg Airport" OR "GSP Airport" OR "GSP airport"
For compound place names, cover both the hyphenated and un-hyphenated forms. Don't assume people type it consistently.
Read the reasoning

Same exact-phrase-variant pattern as Asheville's own boolean. The one new wrinkle: covering both the hyphenated and space-separated forms of a compound place name, plus the common airport-code shorthand people actually use ("GSP Airport").

Custom categories are often reusable, not one-and-done. Worth planning for on bigger name brands especially. A "Fall Seasonal" category built for Trader Joe's (autumn, Halloween, etc.) applies just as well to Costco without rebuilding it from scratch, and the same logic applies to a single ubiquitous brand split across product lines or campaigns (see the scope callout above). Build the taxonomy once per vertical or brand family, not once per search.
Industry Searches

Building an industry / topic boolean

An industry boolean doesn't reference a brand name at all. It's tracking a subject area, usually to give a customer a daily pulse on their space rather than exhaustive coverage. The discipline here is less about disambiguation and more about picking the right topic tags and the right volume-control lever.

Topic + topic intersection

Most industry booleans are two topic tags ANDed together, narrowing to the overlap between the customer's core industry and an adjacent trend or function they care about.

Pick topics deliberately, not adjacently

Choose the second topic tag based on what the customer actually wants to know, not just what's loosely related. "business_news" surfaces corporate moves; a generic finance topic would surface stock-ticker noise the customer doesn't care about.

Geography can replace a second topic

A single topic tag paired with a tight geographic filter can control volume just as well as a topic+topic intersection, especially for inherently local subject areas like K-12 school news.

title: for opportunity-spotting, not just digests

Pairing a topic with title: (matching the headline specifically) surfaces content centrally about the subject, not just mentioning it. That's useful when the goal is spotting marketing or business-development opportunities rather than a daily digest.

OR clusters inside an AND intersection

A single leg of a multi-topic intersection can itself be an OR group of near-synonymous tags, useful when you're not sure which exact topic the relevant content will be classified under, without losing precision on the other legs.

Full list of topic tags: reference the Meltwater topic operator list before picking a pairing. There are more topics available than the handful used in these examples.
Daily digest Venable: AI & Law
Boolean
topic:legal AND topic:machine_learning_and_artificial_intelligence

Filters: US, English, top 10% news (target: under 100/day)

Filters (geography, language, volume percentile) do real work here: they're what keeps a topic+topic boolean at a manageable daily volume.
Read the reasoning

Venable operates in the legal + AI space, so this gives them a pulse on the major daily news at that intersection without needing to read every legal or every AI story.

Daily digest Venable: HR & Law
Boolean
topic:legal AND topic:human_resources

Filters: US, English, top 20% news (target: under 100/day)

The same brand can run multiple topic booleans, one per adjacent space they operate in. They don't need to be combined into one search.
Read the reasoning

Same logic as the AI pairing: Venable is active in HR-related legal work, so this keeps them current on that adjacent space.

Daily digest · deliberate topic choice Trader Joe's: Grocery & Business News
Boolean
topic:food_and_grocery_retailers AND topic:business_news

Filters: US, English, top 10% news

Ask "what does the customer want to know vs. not care about" when picking the second topic. Don't just pattern-match to something adjacent.
Read the reasoning

"business_news" was chosen deliberately, not just as "an adjacent topic." It surfaces corporate and brand-moves coverage, which is what the customer actually wants a pulse on, while excluding noise like generic stock-price ticker news that a broader finance topic would pull in.

Single topic + geo filter Forest Ridge: Local K-12 News
Boolean
topic:primary_and_secondary_schooling_(k-12)

Filters: News, geography narrowed to Seattle and Bellevue only (no second topic tag needed)

If the subject area is inherently local, geography can be your only volume-control lever. You don't always need a second topic.
Read the reasoning

Local K-12 news is inherently a small, geographically bounded pool, so a tight geographic filter does the volume-control work a second topic tag or percentile filter did in the other examples. One topic tag is enough.

Marketing opportunity, not a digest Asheville: Tourism & Marketing Opportunities
Boolean
(topic:tourist_destinations OR topic:specialty_travel) AND title:Asheville
Topic booleans aren't only for "stay informed" digests. They can be purpose-built to surface business-development or marketing opportunities, and title: is a good tool for that.
Read the reasoning

A different "why" than the other topic examples. This isn't a daily pulse-check. It's built to spot marketing and co-marketing opportunities. title: restricts the match to the article's headline, so it surfaces content that's centrally about Asheville as a tourism destination, not just any article that mentions the city in passing.

Daily digest · regulatory angle Health Insurance & Government
Boolean
topic:health_insurance AND topic:government

Filters: US, top 20% news

Same lesson as Trader Joe's business_news pairing: pick the second topic based on the specific angle the customer cares about (here, the regulatory/policy angle), not just a loosely related subject.
Read the reasoning

"government" is the deliberate second topic here, not just "an adjacent topic." The intent is monitoring regulation changes, Medicare and Medicaid updates, and other government-driven health insurance news specifically, rather than general industry news.

Three-topic intersection · OR cluster inside AND ABIM: Training, Health & Policy
Boolean
topic:training_and_certification AND topic:health AND (topic:public_policy OR topic:health_policy OR topic:government)
An AND intersection doesn't have to be one tag per leg. Widen a single leg into an OR cluster of related tags when you're covering a concept that could reasonably be classified several different ways.
Read the reasoning

The industry-monitoring counterpart to ABIM's own brand boolean (see Brand Searches above). Three topics are ANDed together, but the third leg is itself an OR cluster of near-synonymous policy tags (public_policy, health_policy, government) rather than a single pick, useful when you're not confident which exact tag the relevant coverage will be classified under, without giving up precision on the other two legs.

Exclusions

When (and when not) to use NOT

Exclusions are a two-tier decision, not a single technique. And the default isn't a long inline NOT statement in the boolean itself.

Default: the Implementation Cloner System. For common noise types (press releases, stock market and earnings coverage, jobs and hiring content, and a few other standard categories), clone the pre-built custom categories into the customer's account and turn those off at the category level. This is the default path for handling noise; it's faster, more maintainable, and doesn't bloat the boolean itself.
Reserve inline NOT for genuine name collisions. Use a targeted NOT on specific keywords, or NOT topic:, only when there's an actual different business that shares the same or a similar name and operates in a clearly different space. Keep it narrow and specific to that one collision; a long stack of NOT clauses chasing general noise is a sign the exclusion belongs in a custom category instead.
What this looks like in practice: NOT ("Computerised National Identity Card" OR Pakista* OR "CNIC Card"), from the CNIC (Commander, Navy Installations Command) example in Brand Searches above, where "CNIC" also happens to be the common name for Pakistan's national ID card. The NOT is narrow and specific to that one real collision, not a general noise filter. See the full boolean ↑

Before you save it

Quick gut-check

Does the boolean cover realistic name variants: punctuation, spacing, abbreviations, common misspellings?

If the name is generic or ambiguous, is there a disambiguation layer (NEAR/ONEAR + context terms)?

Are exact-match signals included (handle, domain, retail URLs) where relevant?

Does the boolean's scope match the brand's size? Surgical for small/ambiguous, broad catch-all + custom categories for large/ubiquitous.

For topic booleans: is the second topic tag or filter chosen deliberately, based on what the customer actually cares about?

Before adding inline NOT clauses for noise, could the Implementation Cloner System handle it instead?
